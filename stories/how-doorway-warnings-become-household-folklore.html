This record is centered on one tightly linked event chain in plague-era London: an official infected-house mark, a public enforcement rupture, and witnessed door warnings.

The legend form emerges only after that chain is publicly visible and repeatable, not as proof of hidden causes.

Civic orders and the visible threshold

The event begins with a public rule chain in plague-era London. Officials expected infected households to be isolated, and later legal instructions made that rule explicit: a suspected house was to be closed and marked on the door with a red cross and the phrase "Lord have mercy upon us." Warders were assigned to support enforcement and limit contact.

That civic order is the first concrete layer. It is not folklore yet, because the mechanism was bureaucratic: inspection, marking, access control, and penalty language. But this is the same first cause in a legend-origin chain where public instructions later become shared memory.

The St Giles incident: a marked door, then a riot response

On 28 April 1665, in New Buildings in St Giles in the Fields, a house was reported as suspected of plague infection, with a cross and paper fixed to its door. A court-level record shows that those marks were then removed, and the inhabitants were allowed back onto the street in disorderly fashion.

The action stage is narrow and specific:

1) the household was marked as infected, 2) the mark was publicly removed, 3) crowd pressure turned the enforcement moment into a street incident.

That sequence matters because it is one bounded event that could be repeated in memory as "what happened when the doorway warning became contested."

Eyewitness sequence: Pepys and the first visible plague signs

On 7 June 1665, Samuel Pepys recorded what people in the city were seeing: two or three houses with a red cross and the printed warning phrase on their doors. He described this as a first-time sight that changed the emotional state of ordinary movement through the streets.

Cause and action are visible here. The warning had moved from administration into repeated public visibility. A door sign that might have been seen as an isolated control measure was now a recognizable pattern on the urban route itself.

The doorway as warning node and household boundary

The same policy sequence gives the warning strong narrative force:

- A door message made risk legible from the street; - Warders and shut-up rules made that message operational; - Repeated sightings made the message portable from house to house.

Households then retained a practical lesson from this pattern: the marked door became a boundary marker of heightened danger and altered behavior. In family transmission, a public instruction can become an inherited caution phrase.

From administrative mark to folklore template

The strongest transformation happened after the immediate plague wave. The same format—door, short text, visible symbol, constrained movement—was later carried into moral storytelling about entrances as control points. The story form changed, but the core image remained.

That compression created a household warning template:

1) define a threshold, 2) announce caution in a short phrase, 3) treat the threshold as a memory object.

The event itself did not prove supernatural claims, but it produced a repeatable template for domestic warning language.

Q&A

What is the primary historical event in this record?

The 1665 London St Giles doorway incident where a suspected plague house was marked on the door and then removed from the street in a disorderly opening situation, followed by city-wide sightings of marked doors.

Why is this a single-event account?

The narrative is bounded by one documented policy conflict around a specific house marking chain and one eyewitness timeline of how that warning sign then became visible across the city.

What warning format appears in the sources?

A red cross and the phrase 'Lord have mercy upon us' appeared on doors as part of plague containment signaling.

Did the record prove hidden supernatural meaning?

No. It documents a public-health control signal and social response, not a proven supernatural event.

How does this become household folklore?

The visible door signal created a reusable caution format that could be retained in memory as a practical household warning pattern after the plague context passed.

What is the strongest story lesson?

A narrow incident chain can become a durable domestic warning motif when visibility, enforcement, and repeated retelling compress the sequence into a stable template.
